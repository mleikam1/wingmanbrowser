A primary source is evidence from the time or experience you are studying, such as a letter, photograph, map, or interview. It can be valuable without being complete or unbiased.

Use three columns on paper. In the first, record what you observe directly: a date, a printed word, a person standing by a doorway. In the second, record what you think those details might mean. In the third, record questions you would need other evidence to answer.

Ask who created the source, for whom, and for what purpose. Consider what is missing from its view. A photograph can show a crowded street while leaving the photographer's reason for taking it uncertain.

Compare at least two pieces of evidence before making a large claim. If they disagree, describe the disagreement. This activity adapts the Library of Congress approach of observing, reflecting, and questioning in original wording.
