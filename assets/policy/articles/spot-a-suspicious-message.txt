Phishing messages try to persuade people to reveal information, open harmful files, or follow deceptive links. They can arrive by email, text, or another messaging service. A familiar-looking logo alone does not prove that a message is genuine.

Pause when a message creates unusual urgency, asks for private information, or offers something unexpectedly valuable. Do not reply with a password or verification code. Verify a request through a contact method you already know, rather than the link or phone number supplied by the suspicious message.

Use your school or organization's reporting process if one is available. If you already interacted with a suspicious message, tell a trusted adult or your support team promptly so they can help you take the appropriate next steps.

Practice with an invented message on paper: underline the demand, circle the claimed sender, and write what you would verify. Do not collect or share someone else's real messages for this exercise.
